# Lecture Notes: Chapter 14 – Attracting and Retaining Qualified Employees

## 1. Overview
This lecture focuses on how firms attract, motivate, and retain employees through compensation systems and organizational design. Key themes include labor markets, human capital, compensating wage differentials, internal labor markets, promotions, efficiency wages, and salary versus fringe benefits.

---

# 2. Main Notes

## Contracting Objectives

- Firms design compensation contracts to maximize the value of employee output minus costs.
- Compensation systems should align employee interests with firm interests.
- Employees must receive at least their **reservation utility**.

### **Reservation Utility**
- The minimum level of satisfaction/pay an employee requires to accept a job.
- Equivalent to the value of the employee’s next-best alternative.

### Why compensation matters
Compensation is used to:
1. Attract employees
2. Retain employees
3. Motivate employees

---

## The Basic Competitive Model

### Assumptions of the model
- Labor markets are competitive
- Workers and jobs are identical
- Wages are determined by supply and demand
- Compensation is only monetary
- No long-term contracts
- Market wages are easy to observe

The lecture emphasizes that these assumptions are unrealistic but useful as a starting point.

### Firm hiring decision
A firm hires workers until:

**Marginal Revenue Product (MRP) = Market Wage**

### **Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)**
- The additional revenue generated by one more employee.

### Logic
- If MRP > wage → hire more workers
- If MRP < wage → reduce workers

---

## Relaxing the Assumptions

The real world differs from the basic model.

### Important differences
- Jobs are not identical
- Employees are not identical
- Information is costly
- Compensation includes non-monetary elements
- Jobs are often long-term relationships

This creates the need for more advanced compensation systems.

---

## Human Capital

### **Human Capital**
Knowledge, skills, training, and abilities that increase productivity.

### Two types

#### **General Human Capital**
- Useful across many firms
- Example: Excel skills, accounting knowledge
- Employees usually pay for this training themselves

#### **Specific Human Capital**
- Valuable mainly within the current firm
- Example: internal systems or company-specific routines
- Firms are more willing to pay for this training

### Exam relevance
The distinction explains:
- Why firms invest in some training but not others
- Why long-term employment relationships exist

---

## Compensating Wage Differentials

### Definition
Employees must receive higher pay for undesirable jobs.

### Examples of undesirable job characteristics
- Dangerous work
- Night shifts
- Stressful conditions
- Bad work environment

### Key idea
Different workers value job characteristics differently.
Some workers accept lower wages for better conditions.

---

## Internal Labor Markets

### Characteristics
- Entry-level hiring from outside
- Promotions mostly from within
- Long-term employment relationships

### Types of contracts

#### **Explicit Contracts**
- Formal written agreements

#### **Implicit Contracts**
- Informal expectations and understandings

### Why firms use internal labor markets
- Encourages employee loyalty
- Encourages firm-specific human capital investment
- Better matching of workers to jobs over time

---

## Long-Term Relationships (LTR)

### Benefits for firms
- More employee motivation
- More investment in firm-specific skills
- Reduced turnover
- Better employee-job matching

### Key point
Spot-market hiring is often costly in the long run.

---

## Pay in Internal Labor Markets

### Important idea
Firms may:
- Pay employees below MRP early in their career
- Pay employees above MRP later

This creates an incentive to stay with the firm.

### Upward-sloping earnings profile
Salary increases over time due to:
- Greater productivity
- Seniority
- Incentive effects

---

## Efficiency Wages

### Definition
Firms pay wages above market level when monitoring employee effort is difficult.

### Why firms use efficiency wages
- Reduce shirking
- Increase effort and loyalty
- Reduce corruption
- Increase quality

### Chicago Police example
The lecture highlights how high wages combined with penalties reduced corruption.

### Important exam idea
Efficiency wages increase the cost of losing the job, which motivates employees.

---

## Promotions as Tournaments

### Tournament theory
Employees compete against each other for promotions.

### Advantages
- Strong incentives
- Encourages high effort

### Problems
- Reduced cooperation
- Internal politics
- Influence costs
- Employees may value salary more than promotions

### **Peter Principle**
Employees may be promoted until they reach a level where they are incompetent.

---

## Compensation Components

Compensation consists of:
- Salary
- Fringe benefits

### Examples of fringe benefits
- Pension plans
- Insurance
- Healthcare
- Company car
- Extra vacation

### Important point
Salary and fringe benefits are not perfect substitutes.
Employees value them differently.

---

## Salary vs Fringe Benefits

### Why firms offer fringe benefits
- Tax advantages
- Quantity discounts
- Employees value benefits differently

### Optimal mix
The firm wants to:
- Meet employee reservation utility
- At the lowest possible cost

### Economic logic
Optimal compensation occurs where:
- Employee indifference curve is tangent to the firm’s isocost curve.

---

## Payroll Taxes and Compensation

Taxes can change the optimal balance between:
- Salary
- Fringe benefits

If wages are taxed more heavily than benefits, firms may offer more benefits.

---

## Attracting Different Employee Types

Different employees prefer different compensation packages.

### Example
- Single employees may prefer salary
- Married employees may value healthcare and insurance more

### Cafeteria-style benefits
Employees choose among benefit packages.

---

# Lecture Notes: Chapter 15 – Incentive Compensation

## 1. Overview
This lecture examines how firms design compensation systems to motivate employees when interests between owners and employees differ. The lecture focuses on incentive problems, agency theory, risk sharing, principal-agent models, group incentives, and performance-based pay.

---

# 2. Main Notes

## Incentives

### Core problem
Owners and employees have different objectives.

#### Owners
- Want profit maximization
- Want employees to work hard

#### Employees
- Want maximum utility
- Prefer less effort when possible

This creates the **incentive problem**.

---

## The Incentive Problem

### Example: Ian at AssemCo

Ian’s utility:

U = I − e²

Where:
- I = income
- e = effort

### Important insight
Effort creates costs for the employee but benefits for the firm.

### Firm benefit function
Benefits = 100e

### Optimal effort
The firm wants effort where:

Marginal Benefit = Marginal Cost

Result:
- Optimal effort = 50

---

## Problems with Fixed Salaries

The firm cannot perfectly observe effort.

### Problems
- Employees may shirk
- Output may be hard to measure
- External factors affect output

This creates agency problems.

---

## Incentive Problems and Contracts

### Important idea
If employee and firm interests are aligned, incentive problems disappear.

### Contracts can solve problems when:
- Actions are observable
- Performance is measurable

---

## Incentives from Ownership

### Residual Claimancy
Employees receive rights to profits/output.

### Examples
- Franchising
- Managerial buyouts

### Why it works
Employees internalize both:
- Benefits
- Costs

### Limitations
- Risk aversion
- Wealth constraints
- Free-rider problems
- Team production issues

---

## Optimal Risk Sharing

### Key idea
Employees are usually risk-averse.

Employees prefer:
- Stable income
- Less uncertainty

### Shareholders
Shareholders are better able to diversify risk.

### Important implication
From a risk-sharing perspective, employees should receive fixed salaries.

BUT:
- Fixed salaries weaken incentives.

This creates a major trade-off.

---

## Effective Incentive Contracts

### Two important goals
1. Motivate employees
2. Share risk efficiently

### Main trade-off
- Strong incentives = more employee risk
- More insurance/fixed pay = weaker incentives

This trade-off is central to the chapter.

---

## Agency Theory

### Definition
An agency relationship exists when:
- A principal hires an agent to act on their behalf.

### Examples
- Employer → employee
- Shareholders → managers

### Typical assumptions
- Principal is risk neutral
- Agent is risk averse

---

## Basic Principal-Agent Model

### Output function
Q = αe + μ

Where:
- α = marginal productivity
- e = effort
- μ = random factors

### Compensation function
W = W₀ + βQ

Where:
- W₀ = fixed salary
- β = incentive coefficient

### Important idea
Higher β creates stronger incentives.

---

## Employee Effort Choice

### Key result
Increasing:
- Fixed wage → does NOT change effort
- Incentive coefficient β → increases effort

### Why?
Employees respond to marginal rewards, not fixed pay.

---

## Factors Favoring High Incentive Pay

High incentive pay works best when:
- Output strongly depends on effort
- Employees are less risk-averse
- External uncertainty is low
- Performance is easy to measure
- Employees respond strongly to incentives

---

## Informativeness Principle

### Definition
All low-cost information improving performance measurement should be used.

### Important implications
- Include uncontrollable factors
- Improve precision of performance evaluation
- Better measurements reduce inefficient risk bearing

### Example
Relative performance evaluation may remove effects of bad market conditions.

---

## Group Incentive Pay

### When useful
When individual performance is hard to measure.

### Advantages
- Encourages teamwork
- Encourages peer monitoring

### Problems
- Free-rider problem

### Key insight
Team production may create higher total output than separate work.

---

## Forms of Incentive Pay

Examples:
- Piece rates
- Bonuses
- Promotions
- Profit sharing
- Stock ownership
- Preferred assignments
- Firings

### Important point
Rewards do not need to be monetary.

---

## Does Incentive Pay Work?

### Criticism
Some argue money does not motivate.

### Lecture conclusion
Evidence generally shows that incentives affect behavior.

### Important quote
“The problem is not that incentives can’t work but that they work all too well.”

Meaning:
Employees may game poorly designed systems.

---

# 4. Key Concepts

- **Incentive Problem:** Misaligned interests between owners and employees
- **Agency Relationship:** Principal hires agent
- **Risk Aversion:** Preference for stable income
- **Residual Claimancy:** Right to remaining profits
- **Principal-Agent Model:** Framework for incentives and risk sharing

# Lecture Notes: Chapter 16 – Individual Performance Evaluation

## 1. Overview
This lecture examines how firms evaluate employee performance and use performance measures to solve motivation and coordination problems. Key topics include objective and subjective performance measures, relative evaluation, opportunism, balanced scorecards, and team performance.

---

# 2. Main Notes

## Performance Evaluation

### Why firms evaluate performance
1. Provide employee feedback
2. Improve coordination
3. Determine rewards and sanctions
4. Support organizational architecture

### Important principle
Performance measurements must match:
- Compensation systems
- Decision rights

---

## Types of Performance Measurement

### Relative Performance Measures
Employees are evaluated relative to others.

### Absolute Performance Measures
Employees are evaluated independently.

### Objective Measures
Performance criteria are specified in advance and verifiable.

### Subjective Measures
Managers evaluate performance more holistically after the period ends.

---

## Incentive Compensation Model

### Employee output
Q = αe + μ

### Compensation
W = W₀ + βQ

### Key insight
Higher incentive coefficient β creates stronger incentives.

---

## Implicit Assumptions in Performance Measurement

The lecture emphasizes unrealistic assumptions behind simple models:
- Performance easy to measure
- One task/output only
- No gaming
- Output observed cheaply
- Employees work independently

Real organizations are much more complex.

---

## Setting Performance Benchmarks

### Methods
- Time and motion studies
- Past performance
- Standard benchmarks

### Ratchet System
Future targets are based on current performance.

### Ratchet Effect
Employees avoid exceeding targets because future expectations rise.

### Sandbagging
Employees intentionally underperform to create easier future targets.

---

## Measurement Costs

### Key idea
Better measurement systems are costly.

Firms should improve measurements until:

Marginal Benefit = Marginal Cost

---

## Informativeness Principle

### Definition
Use all low-cost information improving assessment of effort.

### Examples
- Market conditions
- Relative benchmarks
- External factors

### Benefits
- More precise evaluation
- Better incentives
- Lower employee risk premium

---

## Opportunism and Gaming

### Gaming
Employees manipulate measures instead of creating value.

### Horizon Problem
Employees focus on short-term results affecting immediate rewards.

### Example
Managers may sacrifice long-term investments to improve short-term bonuses.

---

## Balanced Scorecard

### Purpose
Avoid focusing only on financial performance.

### Four perspectives
1. Financial
2. Customer
3. Internal processes
4. Learning and growth

### Important insight
Using multiple measures reduces opportunism.

---

## Bonus Banks

### Purpose
Reduce short-term opportunism.

### Logic
Bonuses accumulate over time and can later be reduced if performance worsens.

This encourages long-term thinking.

---

## Jensen Critique of Budget-Based Bonuses

### Main argument
The problem is not budgeting itself.

The problem is linking bonuses directly to budgets/targets.

### Consequence
Employees game the system.

### Suggested solution
Linear pay-for-performance systems.

---

## Relative Performance Evaluation

### Definition
Employees are evaluated relative to peers.

### Advantages
- Filters out external shocks
- Better assessment of true effort

### Problems
- Employees may punish high performers
- Incentives to hire weaker coworkers
- Difficult comparisons across firms

---

## Subjective Performance Evaluation

### Why firms use it
Objective measures are imperfect.

Subjective evaluation captures:
- Cooperation
- Leadership
- Mentoring
- Difficult-to-measure behaviors

### Important quote
“You get what you measure.”

Meaning:
Employees focus on measured activities.

---

## Subjective Evaluation Methods

### Standard Rating Scales
Employees ranked on dimensions.

### Goal-Based Systems
Managers evaluate goal achievement.

### Peer Evaluation
Coworkers evaluate each other.

### Problems with peer evaluation
- Friendships
- Rivalries
- Bias

---

## Problems with Subjective Evaluation

### Supervisor Shirking
Managers avoid difficult evaluations.

### Forced Distribution
Employees forced into ranking distributions.

### Influence Costs
Employees spend resources influencing evaluators.

---

## Combining Performance Measures

### Key insight
Neither objective nor subjective measures are perfect.

Combining both usually creates better evaluations.

---

## Team Performance

### Why teams matter
Team production may create synergies.

### Advantages
- Cooperation
- Knowledge sharing
- Complementary skills

### Problems
- Free-riding

### Solution
Peer evaluations and repeated interaction.

---

# 3. Models and Frameworks

## Balanced Scorecard

### What it is
Framework combining financial and non-financial measures.

### Used for
Reducing opportunism and creating balanced evaluation.

### Exam application

# 4. Key Concepts

- **Objective Performance Measure:** Quantifiable and verifiable measure
- **Subjective Performance Measure:** Holistic managerial evaluation
- **Relative Performance Evaluation:** Evaluation relative to peers

- **Ratchet Effect:** Higher future targets after strong performance

# Lecture Notes: Chapter 17 – Divisional Performance Evaluation

## 1. Overview
This lecture focuses on evaluating business units inside firms. It examines cost centers, profit centers, investment centers, transfer pricing, internal accounting, and the distinction between decision management and decision control.

---

# 2. Main Notes

## Measuring Divisional Performance

### Purpose
Business units are evaluated because:
- Rewards depend on performance
- Decision rights must match evaluation systems

### Important principle
Performance measurement must align with:
- Decision rights
- Organizational structure

---

## Cost Centers

### Definition
Units responsible mainly for controlling costs.

### Examples
- Manufacturing
- Service operations

### Objectives
- Minimize cost for a given output
OR
- Maximize output for a given budget

### Important limitation
Cost minimization alone does not guarantee profit maximization.

---

## Expense Centers

### Examples
- HR
- Accounting
- PR
- Research and development

### Characteristics
- Fixed budgets
- Difficult-to-measure output
- Often evaluated subjectively

### Problems
- Overconsumption
- Empire building
- Difficulty measuring productivity

---

## Revenue Centers

### Examples
- Sales departments
- Distribution units

### Objective
Maximize revenue.

### Important problem
Revenue maximization is NOT necessarily profit maximization.

Revenue is maximized where:
MR = 0

Profit is maximized where:
MR = MC

---

## Profit Centers

### Definition
Units responsible for both revenues and costs.

### Managers typically control
- Input mix
- Product mix
- Selling prices

### Advantages
Useful when local knowledge is important.

### Problems
- Externalities between divisions
- Free-riding
- Transfer pricing conflicts
- Overhead allocation issues

---

## Investment Centers

### Definition
Profit centers with authority over capital investments.

### Performance measures
- Return on assets
- Internal rate of return
- Economic Value Added (EVA)

### EVA
Measures value created after accounting for cost of capital.

### Limitation
Traditional accounting measures may discourage long-term investments.

---

## Transfer Pricing

### Definition
Prices charged for internal transfers between divisions.

### Why transfer pricing matters
Transfer prices affect:
1. Profit distribution between units
2. Decision-making incentives
3. Overall company profit

### Central principle
Optimal transfer price = opportunity cost.

---

## Problems in Transfer Pricing

### Asymmetric information
Managers may hide true costs.

### Wrong transfer prices cause
- Bad production decisions
- Bad investment decisions
- Conflicts between divisions

---

## Transfer Pricing Methods

### Opportunity Cost Method
Economically correct but difficult to implement.

### Market-Based Transfer Prices
Use external market prices.

### Marginal Cost Pricing
Transfer price equals marginal cost.

### Problems with marginal cost pricing
- Fixed costs not covered
- Capacity constraints ignored

### Full Cost Pricing
Includes fixed costs.

### Advantages
- Simple
- Easy to calculate

### Risks
- Transfer price may exceed opportunity cost

### Negotiated Prices
Divisions negotiate prices.

### Advantages
Often close to opportunity cost.

### Problems
- Time consuming
- Depends on negotiation skills

---

## Internal Accounting

### Key issue
Accounting systems support:
- Decision management
- Decision control

### Important trade-off
Systems supporting detailed decisions are not always best for control purposes.

---

## Decision Management vs Decision Control

### Decision Management
Initiating and implementing decisions.

### Decision Control
Ratifying and monitoring decisions.

### Four steps
1. Initiation
2. Ratification
3. Implementation
4. Monitoring

---

## Separation Principle

### Rule of thumb
If decision makers do not bear major wealth effects:
- Decision management and control should be separated.

### Why?
Prevents opportunism and agency problems.

---

## Residual Claimant

### Definition
Person with legal rights to profits after all obligations are paid.

### Importance
Residual claimants have strongest incentives to maximize firm value.

---

## Controllability Principle

### Definition
Managers should only be evaluated on factors they can control.

### Importance
Improves fairness and incentive alignment.

---

## Boards of Directors

### Role
Boards:
- Ratify CEO decisions
- Monitor management

Large shareholders also perform monitoring functions.

---

# 4. Key Concepts

- **Cost Center:** Unit responsible for costs
