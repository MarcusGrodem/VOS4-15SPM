# Lecture Notes: Chapter 15 – Incentive Compensation

## 1. Overview
This lecture examines how firms design compensation systems to motivate employees when interests between owners and employees differ. The lecture focuses on incentive problems, agency theory, risk sharing, principal-agent models, group incentives, and performance-based pay.

---

# 2. Main Notes

## Incentives

### Core problem
Owners and employees have different objectives.

#### Owners
- Want profit maximization
- Want employees to work hard

#### Employees
- Want maximum utility
- Prefer less effort when possible

This creates the **incentive problem**.

---

## The Incentive Problem

### Example: Ian at AssemCo

Ian’s utility:

U = I − e²

Where:
- I = income
- e = effort

### Important insight
Effort creates costs for the employee but benefits for the firm.

### Firm benefit function
Benefits = 100e

### Optimal effort
The firm wants effort where:

Marginal Benefit = Marginal Cost

Result:
- Optimal effort = 50

---

## Problems with Fixed Salaries

The firm cannot perfectly observe effort.

### Problems
- Employees may shirk
- Output may be hard to measure
- External factors affect output

This creates agency problems.

---

## Incentive Problems and Contracts

### Important idea
If employee and firm interests are aligned, incentive problems disappear.

### Contracts can solve problems when:
- Actions are observable
- Performance is measurable

---

## Incentives from Ownership

### Residual Claimancy
Employees receive rights to profits/output.

### Examples
- Franchising
- Managerial buyouts

### Why it works
Employees internalize both:
- Benefits
- Costs

### Limitations
- Risk aversion
- Wealth constraints
- Free-rider problems
- Team production issues

---

## Optimal Risk Sharing

### Key idea
Employees are usually risk-averse.

Employees prefer:
- Stable income
- Less uncertainty

### Shareholders
Shareholders are better able to diversify risk.

### Important implication
From a risk-sharing perspective, employees should receive fixed salaries.

BUT:
- Fixed salaries weaken incentives.

This creates a major trade-off.

---

## Effective Incentive Contracts

### Two important goals
1. Motivate employees
2. Share risk efficiently

### Main trade-off
- Strong incentives = more employee risk
- More insurance/fixed pay = weaker incentives

This trade-off is central to the chapter.

---

## Agency Theory

### Definition
An agency relationship exists when:
- A principal hires an agent to act on their behalf.

### Examples
- Employer → employee
- Shareholders → managers

### Typical assumptions
- Principal is risk neutral
- Agent is risk averse

---

## Basic Principal-Agent Model

### Output function
Q = αe + μ

Where:
- α = marginal productivity
- e = effort
- μ = random factors

### Compensation function
W = W₀ + βQ

Where:
- W₀ = fixed salary
- β = incentive coefficient

### Important idea
Higher β creates stronger incentives.

---

## Employee Effort Choice

### Key result
Increasing:
- Fixed wage → does NOT change effort
- Incentive coefficient β → increases effort

### Why?
Employees respond to marginal rewards, not fixed pay.

---

## Factors Favoring High Incentive Pay

High incentive pay works best when:
- Output strongly depends on effort
- Employees are less risk-averse
- External uncertainty is low
- Performance is easy to measure
- Employees respond strongly to incentives

---

## Informativeness Principle

### Definition
All low-cost information improving performance measurement should be used.

### Important implications
- Include uncontrollable factors
- Improve precision of performance evaluation
- Better measurements reduce inefficient risk bearing

### Example
Relative performance evaluation may remove effects of bad market conditions.

---

## Group Incentive Pay

### When useful
When individual performance is hard to measure.

### Advantages
- Encourages teamwork
- Encourages peer monitoring

### Problems
- Free-rider problem

### Key insight
Team production may create higher total output than separate work.

---

## Forms of Incentive Pay

Examples:
- Piece rates
- Bonuses
- Promotions
- Profit sharing
- Stock ownership
- Preferred assignments
- Firings

### Important point
Rewards do not need to be monetary.

---

## Does Incentive Pay Work?

### Criticism
Some argue money does not motivate.

### Lecture conclusion
Evidence generally shows that incentives affect behavior.

### Important quote
“The problem is not that incentives can’t work but that they work all too well.”

Meaning:
Employees may game poorly designed systems.

# 4. Key Concepts

- **Incentive Problem:** Misaligned interests between owners and employees
- **Agency Relationship:** Principal hires agent
- **Risk Aversion:** Preference for stable income
- **Residual Claimancy:** Right to remaining profits
- **Principal-Agent Model:** Framework for incentives and risk sharing
- **Incentive Coefficient (β):** Strength of pay-performance link
- **Informativeness Principle:** Use all useful information in evaluation
- **Free-rider Problem:** Individuals benefit without contributing equally
- **Group Incentives:** Incentives based on team performance
